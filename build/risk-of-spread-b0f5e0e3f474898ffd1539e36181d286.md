:::{hint} At a glance
Nearby countries, particularly Uganda, South Sudan, Rwanda, Kenya, Tanzania, and Zambia, face the highest near-term risk of imported Bundibugyo virus cases from the DRC. Rankings for more distant countries vary across analyses, driven by differences in how each model treats air travel, land travel, and the location of the source cases. Evidence suggests that surveillance is best concentrated on the highest-probability importation routes. Confidence is moderate, with destination rankings holding up better across analyses than the absolute probabilities attached to them, while mobility patterns, case counts, and detection levels remain the main sources of uncertainty.

## Synthesizing evidence

### State of the evidence

As of mid-August, there have been over 4,000 DRC cases, 20 Uganda cases, and one France case [^who-don-1]. The evidence base is broad but still emerging, comprising peer-reviewed risk assessments or reviews, preprints, and one dashboard, with analyses dated from 20 May to 12 August 2026. The evidence base continues to grow, with later analyses reflecting wider DRC spread, Uganda's controlled spread, and airport reopening.[^epistorm-2026-methods-overview-9][^fanelli-2026a-introduction-1]

### How the estimates compare

The studies converge on highlighting significant spillover risks within the region, with various analyses placing Uganda, South Sudan, Rwanda, Kenya, Tanzania, and Zambia among the highest-risk African destinations under multiple scenarios.[^chamla-2026-summary-4][^fanelli-2026a-results-4] The main divergence between analyses is methodological, with air traffic-focused models elevating the ranking of international hubs such as Belgium, France, South Africa, Kenya, and the UAE, whereas land-plus-air models elevate contiguous and near-contiguous African countries.[^kinoshita-2026-abstract-2][^fanelli-2026b-abstract-2] Inside DRC, Okingo et al. show why importation and amplification risk should not be conflated. Areas near the outbreak epicenter rank high for introduction, while high potential for sustained transmission mainly appears in more distant urban centers, including Kinshasa.[^okingo-2026-body-16][^okingo-2026-body-17]

### Key uncertainties and assumptions

A shared challenge is the dependence on mobility data and assumptions of homogeneity in travel behavior[^fanelli-2026a-discussion-3]. Geographic spread estimates hinge on surveillance data quality, which varies[^bouhentala-2026-4-discussion-3][^verheyden-2026-4-discussion-3]. The validity of historical patterns to project future outbreaks under diverse settings adds uncertainty[^van-zandvoort-2026-low-overall-risk-of-exportation-4].

### What would reduce uncertainty

First, verified, onset-dated line lists with health-zone location, travel history, contact status, onset-to-isolation delay and outcome would reduce uncertainty by allowing analyses to distinguish true transmission trends from changing surveillance.[^chamla-2026-discussion-13][^bouhentala-2026-4-discussion-12] Second, would be dynamic mobility data, particularly targeting high-risk corridors in Africa,[^fanelli-2026a-discussion-21] plus reliable air traffic data, since rankings are highly sensitive to cross-border and hub connectivity.[^walekhwa-2026-4-discussion-3][^walekhwa-2026-4-discussion-14] Organizations such as [Flowminder](https://www.flowminder.org/resources/publications-reports/drc-reports-publications) aim to fill this gap by providing more real-time data on mobility trends. Third, comparable assessments of health emergency preparedness across potential destinations, covering laboratory access, infection prevention and control, isolation, contact tracing, and referral pathways, would help, since sustained-transmission risk depends on a destination's containment capacity, not importation alone.[^chamla-2026-discussion-10][^fanelli-2026a-abstract-3]

### Open questions

Several analyses call for regular updating rather than one-off estimates.[^chamla-2026-discussion-13][^walekhwa-2026-4-discussion-14][^fanelli-2026a-discussion-20] They also leave unresolved whether assumptions around early or mild symptomatic travel, humanitarian or charter flights, and changing land mobility would materially shift rankings, and Walekhwa et al. additionally call for refined epidemiological parameter estimates for BDBV, particularly infectious period and serial interval.[^fanelli-2026a-discussion-22][^fanelli-2026a-discussion-6][^walekhwa-2026-4-discussion-15]

### What this means for the response

The evidence suggests emphasizing preparedness in Uganda and South Sudan due to their high importation risks and varied readiness profiles[^gashema-2026-2][^fanelli-2026a-discussion-13]. Ensuring capacity to manage and isolate cases upon detection is necessary[^gashema-2026-13], alongside enhanced cross-border surveillance and testing capacities[^walekhwa-2026-research-in-context-5]. The analyses establish a strong need for ongoing vigilance and rapid response capabilities in these and surrounding countries, allowing for timely adjustments in strategy as new data emerges. For DRC, the evidence suggests maintaining attention beyond the eastern hotspots, including Kinshasa and other urban health zones where amplification potential is high despite distance from the hotspots.[^okingo-2026-body-17] For intercontinental destinations, the evidence suggests focused preparedness, including travel-history recording, diagnostics, isolation capacity, and occupational-exposure protocols, rather than reliance on border screening, because historical exportation outside Africa is rare and mostly occupational or response-linked.[^bouhentala-2026-abstract-2][^bouhentala-2026-abstract-4][^richard-2026-results-2][^van-zandvoort-2026-conclusion-1][^van-zandvoort-2026-discussion-10]

### Verheyden and Mudogo (12 Aug 2026) Incidence-weighted force of infection for predicting first reported health-zone cases during the 2026 Bundibugyo virus disease outbreak: a rolling-origin evaluation

[Read the report](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.08.12.26360244.full-text)

### Overview

This analysis estimates which currently unaffected health zones in four DRC provinces are at highest near-term risk of reporting their first confirmed Bundibugyo virus case, in order to support prepositioning of response capacity. Four connectivity specifications — no connectivity term, road-distance, a gravity score, and an incidence-weighted force-of-infection (FOI) term — were fitted within an identical Bayesian hierarchical hazard architecture across 106 health zones in four affected provinces.[^verheyden-2026-abstract-1] The FOI specification achieved the highest top-10 hit rate (42.6%), though on a global discrimination metric (AUC-PR), a model with no connectivity term performed as well as or better than any connectivity specification (0.437 vs. 0.409 for FOI).[^verheyden-2026-abstract-2]

### Estimate

This analysis does not estimate total outbreak size or case counts; its output is a ranked list of at-risk health zones by their estimated daily hazard of first reporting a confirmed case. The top-ranked zone, Rethy (Ituri), had a posterior median daily hazard of 0.095 (95% CrI [0.018, 0.295]), corresponding to roughly a 75% chance of reporting a first case within 14 days if current conditions persisted; the 20th-ranked zone's daily hazard corresponds to roughly 4% over the same window.[^verheyden-2026-3-results-1] On the pre-specified primary metric (top-10 hit rate), FOI was highest (42.6%), followed by OSRM-distance and gravity (both 32.8%), then baseline (29.5%).[^verheyden-2026-3-results-2] On AUC-PR (a secondary metric), the baseline model with no connectivity term performed as well as or better than every connectivity specification (baseline 0.437 vs. FOI 0.409, gravity 0.399, OSRM-distance 0.239), and FOI's advantage over baseline on this metric was not distinguishable from zero (−0.027, 95% CI [−0.274, 0.200]).[^verheyden-2026-3-results-3] Across the kernel sensitivity grid of 8 alternative combinations, the resulting zone ranking correlated with the primary specification's ranking at Spearman ρ = 0.90–0.99, with top-10 overlap of 9 or 10 out of 10 zones in every combination tested.[^verheyden-2026-3-results-4]

### Methods

**Discrete-time hazard model with incidence-weighted force-of-infection (FOI):** A discrete-time hazard model represents spatial connectivity through the observed incidence in every currently affected zone, propagated to at-risk zones through a road-network distance-decay kernel, referred to as an incidence-weighted spatial force-of-infection (FOI) hazard model.[^verheyden-2026-1-introduction-1] Daily case data come from the INRB-UMIE BDBV2026-Data repository; static covariates include WorldPop population estimates, OpenStreetMap/HOT health-facility points, Complex Crisis Vulnerability Index, and province-level ACLED conflict events; road-network travel times come from a national OSRM graph.[^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-5] CDR/mobility data were not used, as DRC is not among the countries for which this is available.[^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-6] The model is applied to the 106 health zones in Ituri, Nord-Kivu, Haut-Uele, and Tshopo provinces. A sensitivity analysis varied the FOI kernel's decay scale (φ ∈ {30, 60, 120} minutes) and smoothing window (1, 3, 7 days) across a grid of 8 alternative combinations, reporting ranking stability rather than re-selecting a best-performing specification.[^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-7]

**Architecture-matched comparison of four connectivity specifications:** Four specifications of the connectivity covariate were compared within the identical model architecture: (1) a baseline with no connectivity term (static covariates only), (2) road-distance (log travel time to the nearest already-affected zone), (3) a population-weighted gravity score summed over all affected zones, and (4) the incidence-weighted FOI term as the primary specification.[^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-8] Fitting all four within the identical architecture isolates what each connectivity term contributes beyond the static covariates and beyond each other.[^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-9]

**Rolling-origin pseudo-prospective evaluation:** Eight weekly origins from 21 May to 9 July 2026 were used, each refit on data available through that origin and scored against zones reporting a first case within the following 14 days; top-10 hit rate — the proportion of true next-invading zones ranked in the model's top 10 — was pre-specified as the primary metric.[^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-10] Uncertainty quantification used a cluster bootstrap resampling whole origins with replacement (10,000 resamples).[^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-11] Two further robustness checks were applied: leave-one-origin-out and a non-overlapping-origin subset of 4 origins spaced exactly 14 days apart.[^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-12]

**Exploratory growth-rate extension for already-affected zones:** A secondary exploratory analysis examined near-term case growth for the 47 already-affected zones using a hierarchical negative-binomial growth-rate model, after an initial Poisson specification was found miscalibrated on rolling-origin backtesting.[^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-13] This is not presented as a second validated model of comparable standing, and a combined invasion-plus-growth ranking is not presented as a validated decision tool, as that combined ranking has not itself been backtested.[^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-14]

### Assumptions

**FOI kernel parameters:** The FOI term uses a distance-decay kernel K(z,z') = exp(−d(z,z')/φ) with φ = 60 minutes as the default decay scale, and a 3-day trailing mean of new confirmed cases through day t−2 as the incidence weight.[^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-19] The decay scale and smoothing window were not tuned against performance; across a grid of 8 alternative combinations (φ ∈ {30, 60, 120} minutes × window ∈ {1, 3, 7} days), the resulting zone ranking correlated with the primary specification at Spearman ρ = 0.90–0.99, with top-10 overlap of 9 or 10 out of 10 zones in every combination, indicating the ranking is stable across plausible kernel choices.[^verheyden-2026-3-results-5]

**Temporal lag to avoid leakage:** Same-day leakage is addressed by using only incidence through day t−2, never day t or t−1, to construct the FOI covariate.[^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-20] An earlier, same-day (non-lagged) specification yielded a FOI coefficient of +0.610 [0.43, 0.79] compared to +0.357 [0.16, 0.55] after applying the lag, indicating same-day leakage had inflated the apparent effect by roughly 40%.[^verheyden-2026-3-results-6]

**Outcome definition:** The outcome modelled is a zone's first reported confirmed case, not the date of biological introduction of the virus; first confirmation reflects viral introduction, local transmission, detection, testing access, reporting capacity, and retrospective case reclassification jointly, and the model cannot separate these.[^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-21]

**Static covariates and structural form:** Health-facility density (β = +0.486, [0.245, 0.734]) and population (β = +0.400, [0.087, 0.720]) are associated with hazard at the 95% credible level and are included as structural inputs; socioeconomic deprivation (+0.326, [−0.074, 0.754]) and province-level conflict exposure (+0.156, [−0.640, 0.900]) were not distinguishable from zero.[^verheyden-2026-3-results-7] Conflict exposure is measured only at province level across four provinces, alongside a province random effect estimated from the same four groups; this covariate's estimate is described as exploratory.[^verheyden-2026-4-discussion-2]

**Data-vintage leakage and pseudo-prospective evaluation:** Data-vintage leakage — whether case counts available for a historical date already reflect corrections made after that date — cannot be ruled out, since only the final, most recently corrected dataset is held rather than dated historical snapshots; the rolling-origin evaluation is therefore described as pseudo-prospective throughout, not a true contemporaneous test.[^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-22]

**Province random effect:** A non-centred province random effect is included in the linear predictor alongside a piecewise-constant weekly baseline evolving as a random walk and weakly informative priors.[^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-23]

### Limitations

**Pseudo-prospective evaluation and data-vintage leakage:** Because historical snapshots of the surveillance dataset at each rolling-origin date were not available, the authors cannot rule out that retrospective data revision is reflected in what the evaluation treats as contemporaneously known information; the rolling-origin evaluation is therefore described as pseudo-prospective throughout, reserving the term prospective for the genuinely pre-registered future verification.[^verheyden-2026-1-introduction-2]

**Outcome reflects detection as well as introduction:** The outcome modelled is first reported confirmed case, not the unobserved date of viral introduction; these differ to the extent that introduction, local transmission, detection, testing access, and reporting capacity differ across zones, and the model cannot separate these mechanisms. This matters specifically for the health-facility-density covariate, whose positive association with hazard may partly or wholly reflect greater detection capacity in better-served zones rather than, or in addition to, genuinely greater introduction risk.[^verheyden-2026-4-discussion-3]

**Absence of mobile-phone mobility data:** Existing approaches to spatial spread typically rely on mobile-phone call detail record data, which a systematic review found available in only a small number of African countries, not including DRC; CDR data were therefore not used in this analysis.[^verheyden-2026-1-introduction-3][^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-6]

**Statistical uncertainty in the primary comparison:** The top-10 hit rate comparison against road-distance reaches only the boundary of conventional significance (CI lower bound 0.000); the authors state they would not describe this as a robust result on its own and rely on convergent evidence across the primary metric, the sensitivity grid, and the leave-one-origin-out and non-overlapping-origin checks collectively.[^verheyden-2026-4-discussion-4]

**AUC-PR divergence:** On AUC-PR, the baseline model with no connectivity term performed as well as or better than every connectivity specification; the authors report this plainly, noting that a global discrimination metric rewards correctly ranking the bulk of zones that will not invade soon, which static covariates alone appear to do reasonably well, while the connectivity terms' distinguishable contribution is specifically to sharpening the top of the ranking.[^verheyden-2026-3-results-8]

**Conflict exposure measured only at province level:** Conflict exposure is measured only at province level across four provinces, alongside a province random effect estimated from the same four groups; the authors state this covariate's estimate should be read as exploratory.[^verheyden-2026-4-discussion-2]

**Growth-rate extension not combined with hazard model:** The growth-rate extension has not been backtested as a combined ranking with the hazard model, and the authors do not present one for that reason.[^verheyden-2026-4-discussion-5]

**Fixed modelling choices not evaluated:** Fixed choices not evaluated in this analysis include the OSRM travel-time transformation and the province-level (rather than zone-level) conflict aggregation.[^verheyden-2026-4-discussion-6]

### Data sources

**Primary outbreak data:** Daily case data come from the INRB-UMIE BDBV2026-Data repository (Mbulayi et al., 2026).[^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-15] The study period covers 14 May to 26 July 2026, yielding 5,727 zone-day observations and 47 first-case events across the risk set.[^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-1][^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-16]

**Auxiliary data:** Static covariates include WorldPop population estimates, OpenStreetMap/HOT health-facility points, Complex Crisis Vulnerability Index, and province-level ACLED conflict events.[^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-17] Road-network travel times were derived from a national OSRM graph.[^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-18] CDR/mobility data were not used, as DRC is not among the countries for which such data are available.[^verheyden-2026-2-materials-and-methods-6] An independent Flowminder-based mobility analysis (Bangelesa et al., 2026, as summarised in Walekhwa et al., 2026) found the outbreak's three epicentre zones among the top 5% of DRC health zones nationally on mobility intensity; this was used as external, indirect support for the general connectivity premise, not as a model input.[^verheyden-2026-4-discussion-1]

### Epistorm (23 Jul 2026) Ebola Bundibugyo Virus — DRC · Spread Risk Assessment · International Dissemination

[Read the report](https://epistorm.github.io/EBV2026/Report6RR.html)

### Overview

This analysis is not an outbreak-size estimate but a geographic risk assessment: it estimates where an exported BVD case is most likely to be detected internationally, conditional on at least one export occurring. The method combines air-travel and short-scale commuting flows into a "mobility-only relative importation risk (RR)" using the GLEAM (Global Epidemic and Mobility) framework, evaluated over a two-week horizon, with no transmission model used at this stage; the analysis describes where any exported case is most likely to be detected, conditional on at least one export occurring[^epistorm-2026-executive-summary-1]. The headline finding is that proximity spillover concentrates in western Uganda and Juba, with 99.2% of the city-level relative-risk mass across neighbouring countries sitting in five cities: Kasese (Uganda, 56.3%), Pakuba (Uganda, 25.4%), Arua (Uganda, 12.8%), Juba (South Sudan, 3.1%), and Entebbe (Uganda, 1.5%)[^epistorm-2026-key-findings-1], and that once Uganda is removed, South Sudan takes 80.1% of the tail (Juba alone 79.6%)[^epistorm-2026-key-findings-2].

### Estimate

This analysis does not produce an outbreak-size estimate; it produces a conditional destination-risk ranking. At the neighbouring-country/city level, the mobility model concentrates 99.2% of the non-DRC relative risk mass in a small number of cities on the eastern side of the country: Kasese, Pakuba, Arua, Entebbe (Uganda) and Juba (South Sudan)[^epistorm-2026-neighbouring-country-cities-2], with Uganda alone accounting for 96.1% of the non-DRC mass[^epistorm-2026-neighbouring-country-cities-3]. After removing Uganda and renormalising the remaining 117 destinations, the tail is dominated by South Sudan: Juba alone carries 79.6%, and Rumbek adds a further 0.5%, reflecting the strong overland connectivity from north-eastern DRC into South Sudan captured by the mobility model[^epistorm-2026-regional-distribution-international-tail-1]. Regionally, Africa carries 89.4% of the tail (South Sudan alone 80.1%; then Kenya 2.8%, Tanzania 1.1%, Rwanda 0.8%, South Africa 0.8%, Ethiopia 0.7%), the Middle East accounts for 4.9% (UAE 3.2%, Saudi Arabia 0.9%, Qatar 0.3%), Europe for 3.0% (United Kingdom 1.0%, Germany 0.3%, Belgium 0.3%), Asia for 2.0% (India 0.9%, China 0.7%), and the Americas for 0.7%[^epistorm-2026-regional-distribution-international-tail-2]. The authors emphasize that the absolute probability of any single international export remains small[^epistorm-2026-executive-summary-6], and that these are conditional shares, not absolute probabilities (representing the share of international export probability that would land in each destination if an international export were to occur, not the absolute probability that an export will occur[^epistorm-2026-executive-summary-7]).

### Methods

**Mobility-only relative-risk (RR) model (GLEAM framework):** The analysis models importation risk by combining global air travel patterns from IATA/OAG passenger-level origin-destination flows with short-scale commuting patterns capturing daily movements between adjacent subpopulations, following the GLEAM (Global Epidemic and Mobility) computational framework[^epistorm-2026-methods-overview-1]. For each candidate destination, the model estimates the probability that an infected individual originating in the affected area travels there, conditional on at least one exported case occurring, producing a relative risk distribution normalized so that values represent the share of importation probability assigned to each location[^epistorm-2026-methods-overview-2]. The method is applied at two geographic scales as two "views" over the same RR distribution: a city-level view across DRC's neighbouring countries before renormalisation, showing Uganda alone accounts for 96.1% of the non-DRC mass[^epistorm-2026-methods-overview-3], and a second view that removes only Uganda and renormalises the remaining mass over 117 international destinations reachable by air[^epistorm-2026-methods-overview-4]. No sensitivity analyses varying model inputs are reported; the framework itself is stated to be unchanged from prior reports (the framework is unchanged from Reports #1 to #4[^epistorm-2026-executive-summary-5]).

### Assumptions

**Mobility-only, no transmission dynamics:** The model treats importation purely as a function of travel mobility; the report uses the mobility framework only, with no transmission-dynamics model applied, and RR values should not be read as forecasts of future case counts at any destination[^epistorm-2026-limitations-assumptions-1].

**No border-control interventions:** The mobility model does not simulate country-level border-control interventions such as entry screening, restrictions on travellers from the affected area, or air-service suspensions[^epistorm-2026-methods-overview-7][^epistorm-2026-limitations-assumptions-2], so RR values are described as a mobility-only baseline[^epistorm-2026-methods-overview-8][^epistorm-2026-limitations-assumptions-3].

**Incubation period constraint:** The incubation period of Bundibugyo virus (2-21 days; average ~10 days) constrains the window within which an exported case could reasonably travel while infectious or pre-symptomatic.[^epistorm-2026-limitations-assumptions-4]

**Independence and homogeneity of travellers:** Case importations are modelled as statistically independent events.[^epistorm-2026-limitations-assumptions-5] The model also does not incorporate sociodemographic attributes of travellers (age, economic status, occupation, or pre-existing medical conditions); travel probability within a catchment area is treated as independent of these attributes and of specific location within the catchment[^epistorm-2026-limitations-assumptions-6], and estimates apply to traffic from the general population[^epistorm-2026-limitations-assumptions-7], excluding repatriation flights, responder evacuations, or other ad-hoc operational movements[^epistorm-2026-limitations-assumptions-8].

### Limitations

**No border-control modelling affects absolute but not conditional risk:** The mobility model does not simulate country-level border-control interventions such as entry screening, restrictions on travellers, or air-service suspensions; any such measure would in particular reduce the absolute probability of an international export, while the conditional ranking of where an export would land is less sensitive but still affected.[^epistorm-2026-methods-overview-9]

**Informal cross-border movement undercaptured:** Mobility data may not fully capture informal cross-border movement, which is substantial within the immediate cross-border region but is not the focus of the international-tail analysis in Sections 4 and 5.[^epistorm-2026-limitations-assumptions-9]

**No downstream transmission modelling:** The model does not predict downstream transmission chains following an importation event: risk estimates reflect the probability of a single importation and do not account for the probability of sustained local transmission in the receiving location.[^epistorm-2026-limitations-assumptions-10]

**Clustered travel undercounts exposure events:** Real-world events involving multiple related cases (for example, a family cluster travelling together) are counted as a single importation event; to the extent that such clusters are common, the model will under-count the number of distinct exposure events while correctly counting the number of distinct geographic seedings.[^epistorm-2026-limitations-assumptions-11]

**Sociodemographic and operational-travel exclusions:** The model does not incorporate sociodemographic attributes of travellers, and the importation risk associated with repatriation flights, responder evacuations, or other ad-hoc operational movements is not part of this analysis.[^epistorm-2026-limitations-assumptions-12][^epistorm-2026-limitations-assumptions-8]

### Data sources

Primary outbreak data: 23 July 2026 DRC data[^epistorm-2026-executive-summary-2] underlying the refreshed assessment. Auxiliary data: global air travel patterns derived from the International Air Transport Association (IATA) and the Official Airline Guide (OAG), providing passenger-level origin-destination flows between international airports[^epistorm-2026-methods-overview-5], complemented by short-scale commuting patterns capturing daily movements between adjacent subpopulations, representing the local spread of disease through routine human activity within and across health zones[^epistorm-2026-methods-overview-6]. The incubation period parameter is drawn from the historical 2007 outbreak (2-21 days, average ~10 days, from the 2007 outbreak[^epistorm-2026-situation-at-a-glance-2]), and pathogen background notes Bundibugyo virus, second Ebola species, first isolated 2007 in Uganda, confirmed CFR ~14% (DRC/UGA combined)[^epistorm-2026-situation-at-a-glance-3].

### Bouhentala & Kadir (15 Jul 2026) Epidemiological Analysis of the 2026 Bundibugyo Virus Disease Outbreak and Rapid Risk Assessment for North Africa and Europe

[Read the report](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.19.26358411)

### Overview

This analysis assesses the risk of introduction and onward transmission of the 2026 BDBV outbreak into North Africa and Europe, using publicly available aggregate surveillance data synthesized through mid-July 2026. Risk was assessed across four dimensions: introduction likelihood, conditional onward-transmission likelihood, impact, and confidence.[^bouhentala-2026-abstract-1] Introduction likelihood was assessed as very low to low for North Africa and very low for the general European population, with delayed recognition in routine healthcare identified as the principal scenario for limited secondary transmission.[^bouhentala-2026-abstract-2]

### Estimate

This analysis does not produce a quantitative outbreak-size estimate for DRC. It reports observed surveillance counts as descriptive figures rather than model-derived estimates. As of 15 July 2026, the DRC had reported 2,124 laboratory-confirmed cases, 828 deaths among confirmed cases, 390 reported recoveries, and 725 patients hospitalised in isolation, across 46 of 140 health zones in five provinces.[^bouhentala-2026-3-results-4] The headline epidemiological trend finding is a notification-rate ratio: the aggregate rate was 35.9 per day during 2–29 June (970 notifications over 27 days; exact counting interval 33.7–38.3) and 47.4 per day during 1–15 July (664 over 14 days; 43.9–51.2), yielding a July-to-June notification-rate ratio of 1.32 (counting-model 95% interval 1.20–1.46).[^bouhentala-2026-3-results-5] For importation risk, the analysis cites an external ECDC estimate rather than producing its own: ECDC estimated approximately one importation per 23,000–24,000 travellers from the principal outbreak region (90% uncertainty interval one per 13,000–54,000), and under a hypothetical scenario of 100 travellers during 11–25 June, estimated a 0.45% probability of at least one importation (90% uncertainty interval 0.20%–0.85%).[^bouhentala-2026-3-results-6]

### Methods

**Rapid public-health risk assessment using aggregate surveillance data:** This was a rapid public-health analysis of publicly available aggregate surveillance data and official risk assessments, drawing on WHO Disease Outbreak News, WHO/IHR statements and technical guidance, ECDC surveillance and modelling reports, WHO regional offices, national ministry-of-health dashboards, and peer-reviewed primary studies for historical BDBV outbreaks, viral persistence, and candidate countermeasures.[^bouhentala-2026-2-methods-4] Airline sources were used only to establish the existence of plausible routes and not to estimate passenger volumes.[^bouhentala-2026-2-methods-5] This approach covers DRC nationally (with provincial detail for Ituri) as the source population, and North Africa and Europe as destination geographies.

**Interval-specific notification-rate analysis:** Cumulative confirmed DRC counts were indexed by reporting date rather than symptom-onset date, and interval-specific average notification rates were calculated as the change in cumulative confirmed cases divided by the number of days between reports, with exact 95% Poisson counting intervals calculated using the Garwood method.[^bouhentala-2026-2-methods-6] The notification-rate ratio comparing 1–15 July with 2–29 June was calculated on the log scale using SE[log(RR)] = √(1/C₁ + 1/C₀).[^bouhentala-2026-2-methods-7] No reproduction number was estimated because onset-date line-list data were unavailable.[^bouhentala-2026-2-methods-8]

**Structured qualitative risk rating:** Risk was separated into four dimensions — introduction likelihood, conditional onward-transmission likelihood after an importation, impact if detection was delayed, and confidence in the judgement — with categories treated as comparative reference classes rather than calibrated probabilities; boundary ratings such as 'very low to low' were retained when the evidence did not support a more precise classification.[^bouhentala-2026-2-methods-9] The ratings were calibrated against WHO's 6 June assessment, which classified risk as very high in the DRC, high in Uganda and bordering countries, low in the remainder of the WHO African Region, and low globally.[^bouhentala-2026-2-methods-10]

### Assumptions

**Reporting-date indexing and its limitations:** The Poisson counting intervals describe counting variation only and do not incorporate systematic uncertainty from backlogs, under-ascertainment, changing testing, or reporting delays.[^bouhentala-2026-2-methods-13] The observed increase in notification rates cannot be interpreted as a direct measure of biological acceleration, as WHO noted that expanded surveillance, testing, and diagnostic capacity contributed to rising reported counts, and newly reported cases could include backlogs.[^bouhentala-2026-3-results-11]

**Risk ratings as comparative reference classes rather than calibrated probabilities:** Risk categories are comparative reference classes rather than calibrated probabilities.[^bouhentala-2026-2-methods-14] Passenger-volume and confidential readiness data were unavailable[^bouhentala-2026-3-results-12], so country ratings rest on route availability, occupational exposure patterns, and qualitative preparedness indicators rather than quantified traveller volumes.

**Transmission requires direct contact:** A recognised symptomatic case can usually be contained because transmission requires direct contact with blood or other body fluids, contaminated materials, or the body of a deceased case rather than ordinary casual proximity.[^bouhentala-2026-4-discussion-1] This structural assumption underpins the low conditional sustained-transmission rating for North Africa and Europe.

**Border screening ineffectiveness during incubation:** An incubating traveller can cross a border while asymptomatic because people are not considered infectious before symptom onset and the incubation period can extend to 21 days; temperature screening may detect some symptomatic travellers but cannot exclude infection during incubation.[^bouhentala-2026-4-discussion-2]

**Itinerary concealment risk:** A final departure airport may conceal earlier travel to eastern DRC; triage therefore requires a complete 21-day itinerary and exposure history.[^bouhentala-2026-3-results-13]

**Cumulative doubling time not treated as a transmission signal:** Arithmetic cumulative doubling times were treated as descriptive properties of the cumulative curve and not as evidence of changes in transmission.[^bouhentala-2026-2-methods-15]

### Limitations

**Dependence on public aggregate data:** The main limitation is dependence on public aggregate data; reporting dates do not represent symptom onset, and the analysis could not estimate time-varying reproduction numbers, serial intervals, onset-to-isolation delays, household secondary-attack rates, health-zone-specific growth, or intervention effects.[^bouhentala-2026-4-discussion-3]

**Incomplete auxiliary inputs for importation assessment:** Passenger volumes, traveller occupations, deployment rosters, medevac activity, and country-specific laboratory and referral readiness were incomplete; country ratings were therefore comparative judgements rather than calibrated probabilities.[^bouhentala-2026-4-discussion-4]

**Counting intervals understating true uncertainty:** Notification rates remain composite measures of infection, care seeking, alert investigation, specimen transport, laboratory capacity, retrospective death investigation, and reporting; the exact Poisson intervals are therefore deliberately described as counting-model intervals rather than complete epidemiological uncertainty intervals.[^bouhentala-2026-4-discussion-5]

**CFR interpretation caution:** The rise in confirmed-case CFR is compatible with maturation of outcomes among previously active cases, retrospective confirmation of deaths, delayed presentation, preferential detection of severe disease, delayed recovery reporting, and changes in testing or death investigation; it should not be interpreted as a time-resolved measure of clinical virulence.[^bouhentala-2026-3-results-14]

**Unresolved status discrepancy in DRC counts:** The published status totals did not fully reconcile — 828 deaths, 390 recoveries, and 725 hospitalised patients summed to 1,943, leaving 181 of 2,124 confirmed cases outside those categories — and public reports did not define the status of these cases; potential explanations include pending admission or outcome reconciliation, transfers, and reporting lags, but no category could be assigned without line-list data.[^bouhentala-2026-3-results-15]

**Time-stamped analysis:** The analysis was time-stamped and may be superseded by subsequent official reports.[^bouhentala-2026-4-discussion-6]

### Data sources

**Primary outbreak data:** Public surveillance reports from WHO, ECDC, Africa CDC, national ministries of health, and peer-reviewed sources were synthesised through 15–17 July 2026.[^bouhentala-2026-abstract-3] The main DRC trajectory used laboratory-confirmed cases and deaths among confirmed cases; suspected, probable, and confirmed categories were not summed across reports because individuals and deaths can be reclassified as investigations progress.[^bouhentala-2026-2-methods-11] Uganda's official dashboard as of 17 July reported 20 confirmed cases, two confirmed deaths, 18 recoveries, and no current admissions.[^bouhentala-2026-3-results-7] WHO's jurisdictional accounting on 17 July comprised 2,145 confirmed cases globally: 2,124 in the DRC, 20 in Uganda, and one in France, with 830 confirmed-case deaths.[^bouhentala-2026-3-results-8]

**Auxiliary data:** Peer-reviewed primary studies were used for historical BDBV outbreaks, viral persistence, and candidate countermeasures.[^bouhentala-2026-2-methods-12] Airline sources were used only to establish the existence of plausible routes and not to estimate passenger volumes.[^bouhentala-2026-2-methods-5] ECDC Monte Carlo importation summaries (approximately one importation per 23,000–24,000 travellers, with 90% uncertainty interval one per 13,000–54,000) were referenced as separately generated estimates and were not treated as exact reciprocals.[^bouhentala-2026-3-results-9][^bouhentala-2026-3-results-10]

:::{tab-item} Fanelli et al. (5 Jul 2026)
### Fanelli et al. (5 Jul 2026) Importation risk and preparedness priorities across Africa in the 2026 Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak

[Read the report](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.21.26358330)

### Overview

This analysis estimates the risk of Bundibugyo Ebola virus disease (BVD) importation from the ongoing outbreak in eastern DRC and Uganda to all other African countries, and contextualizes that risk against each country's epidemic readiness. The authors developed an integrated spatial framework combining high-resolution epidemic and population data, air and land mobility, geographical accessibility, and conflict-adjusted travel times, then contextualized importation risk using national readiness indicators.[^fanelli-2026a-abstract-1] Importation risk was highly geographically concentrated, with neighbouring countries accounting for 85% of continental risk; South Sudan, Rwanda, Kenya, Tanzania, Zambia, Ethiopia, Burundi, Angola, South Africa, the Central African Republic, and Nigeria were estimated at high risk, with the largest readiness gaps in South Sudan, Burundi, and the Central African Republic.[^fanelli-2026a-abstract-2]

### Estimate

This analysis does not estimate total outbreak size (case counts). Its primary output is relative importation risk — the estimated probability that an infected traveller from the affected areas reaches each African country — stratified by destination country, mobility pathway, and readiness. Key findings are as follows.

The 11 countries in the high-risk quartile — South Sudan, Rwanda, Kenya, Zambia, Ethiopia, Tanzania, Burundi, Angola, South Africa, Central African Republic, and Nigeria — accounted for 93% of the total estimated importation risk, forming a largely contiguous regional belt surrounding the affected area, with Ethiopia, South Africa, and Nigeria at greater geographic distance (border distance 2, 3, and 3 from the outbreak source, respectively).[^fanelli-2026a-results-1]

Neighbouring countries alone accounted for 85% of the total estimated risk, while countries located two border crossings away contributed an additional 9%.[^fanelli-2026a-results-2]

Ethiopia, South Africa, and Nigeria were the only non-neighbouring countries classified as high risk overall; each ranked substantially higher for air importation risk than land importation risk — Ethiopia ranked first for air but fifth for land, South Africa third and ninth, and Nigeria seventh and eleventh.[^fanelli-2026a-results-3]

Under amplification scenarios, the ranking of high-risk countries remained largely unchanged across the seven scenarios tested, with South Sudan, Rwanda, Kenya, Tanzania, and Zambia remaining among the five countries at highest risk.[^fanelli-2026a-results-4] Amplification in Nairobi and Lusaka produced the largest increase in continental importation (4-fold), and up to 15-fold in southern Africa; amplification in Kampala and Kigali produced a moderate increase (2.5-fold and 2.8-fold, respectively), whereas expansion to Kinshasa, Bujumbura, or Juba produced comparatively limited continental effects.[^fanelli-2026a-results-5]

### Methods

**Integrated multi-layer importation risk model:** The authors developed an integrated analytical framework combining epidemiological and high-resolution spatial population data, commercial air transportation, land mobility, geographical accessibility, conflict-adjusted travel times, and different readiness scores to estimate the risk of Ebola Bundibugyo dissemination across Africa.[^fanelli-2026a-introduction-2] Importation risk for each African country was estimated separately for air and land mobility as the probability that an infected traveller from the affected areas would reach that country, assuming infected cases could travel only during the latent (pre-symptomatic) phase; for land mobility, travel times were incorporated to estimate the destination reached before symptom onset, while air journey times were considered negligible relative to the incubation period.[^fanelli-2026a-methods-4] Risks were weighted by the cumulative attack rate in each affected administrative unit, and air and land importation risks were then combined into an overall relative importation risk per destination country.[^fanelli-2026a-methods-5]

**Air mobility component:** Air mobility was derived from IATA data on origin–destination passenger flows between any pair of commercial airports, accounting for connections at intermediate airports.[^fanelli-2026a-methods-6] Population in each administrative unit was assigned to airports using weights proportional to population size and decreasing with distance to each airport.[^fanelli-2026a-methods-7]

**Land mobility component:** Land mobility was obtained as residual mobility by subtracting air mobility from the Global Transnational Mobility Dataset 2.0 (GTMD2), which provides estimates of overall population movements between countries.[^fanelli-2026a-methods-8] To characterize spatial accessibility and potential for land-based spread within the incubation period, travel-time surfaces were computed using a global motorized-travel friction surface integrating road networks, land cover, rivers, topography, and transport infrastructure, estimated from each affected health district and ADM3 unit to all destination countries, and adjusted for conflict-related disruptions using ACLED data calibrated against Google Maps driving-time estimates.[^fanelli-2026a-methods-9] The share of population in each unit travelling to each destination country was estimated through a gravity model calibrated on observed land mobility patterns.[^fanelli-2026a-methods-10] Sensitivity analyses reduced land mobility by 66% and 90% and varied assumed daily driving time beyond the baseline of 8 hours; these produced only limited changes in country rankings and no changes in the estimated geography of risk.[^fanelli-2026a-discussion-1]

**Readiness contextualization:** National epidemic readiness was assessed using the WHO SPAR (2025), the Global Health Security Index (GHS, 2021), the Infectious Disease Vulnerability Index (IDVI, 2016), and the Epidemic Risk Index (INFORM-WHO, 2020), with indicators on healthcare system capacity and emergency preparedness and response extracted from each framework.[^fanelli-2026a-methods-11] SPAR was selected as the primary readiness metric given its complete coverage of African countries, recent update, and direct relevance to the WHO Framework for Health Emergency Preparedness and Response Capabilities.[^fanelli-2026a-methods-12] GHS indicators were used in sensitivity analyses.[^fanelli-2026a-methods-13]

**Amplification scenarios:** Amplification scenarios were designed to assess potential impact of epidemic expansion beyond the currently affected area into highly connected urban centres, including the capital cities of DRC (Kinshasa) and Uganda (Kampala), and the capitals of neighbouring high-risk countries: Juba, Kigali, Bujumbura, Nairobi, and Lusaka.[^fanelli-2026a-methods-14]

**Reporting-rate adjustment:** To account for geographically heterogeneous reporting rates, the authors used the proportion of alerts investigated among reported alerts in each DRC province, averaged over the two weeks preceding 5 July 2026, relative to 100% reporting in Uganda, and explored alternative values in sensitivity analysis.[^fanelli-2026a-methods-15]

### Assumptions

**Latent-phase travel only:** The analysis assumed that Ebola-infected cases could travel only during their latent (pre-symptomatic) phase, as symptomatic patients would be expected to either remain in the affected country for clinical management or, if transferred internationally, travel under medically supervised and controlled conditions.[^fanelli-2026a-methods-21] This is consistent with the rapid onset of symptoms and progressive clinical deterioration characteristic of Ebola virus disease; however, the authors acknowledge that some individuals with early or mild symptoms may still travel before seeking care, particularly over shorter distances, potentially increasing importation risk to more distant destinations.[^fanelli-2026a-discussion-2]

**Homogeneous travel behaviour:** The analysis assumed homogeneous travel behaviour and exposure risk across travellers, although age, occupation, socioeconomic conditions, purpose of travel, and individual exposure may modify infection risk, as illustrated by the imported case in France involving a medical doctor working in the response.[^fanelli-2026a-discussion-3]

**Reporting rate proxy:** To account for geographically heterogeneous under-ascertainment, the proportion of alerts investigated among reported alerts in each DRC province over the two preceding weeks was used as a proxy for local reporting rates, relative to 100% reporting assumed for Uganda; alternative values were explored in sensitivity analysis.[^fanelli-2026a-methods-15] Geographically heterogeneous under-ascertainment is difficult to quantify, though the results were found to be robust to alternative assumptions.[^fanelli-2026a-discussion-4]

**Land travel duration and daily driving time:** Land-mediated importation risk was estimated assuming an average travel duration of 8 hours per day; alternative travel durations produced no changes in the estimated geography of risk.[^fanelli-2026a-discussion-5]

**Border restrictions excluded:** Border restrictions and closures related to the current outbreak were not modelled because implemented measures were partial, temporary, and largely limited to official points of entry, and available cross-country mobility data suggested no meaningful reduction in cross-border movement occurred during such restrictions.[^fanelli-2026a-methods-22]

**Air travel restricted to commercial passenger flights:** Air-mediated importation risk only considered commercial passenger flights and did not account for humanitarian, military, or charter flights.[^fanelli-2026a-discussion-6]

**Attack rate weighting:** Importation risks were weighted by the cumulative attack rate in each affected administrative unit[^fanelli-2026a-methods-23], meaning areas with higher local epidemic intensity contribute proportionally more to estimated exportation risk.

**SPAR as primary readiness metric:** SPAR was selected as the primary epidemic readiness metric because of its complete coverage of African countries, recent update (2025), and direct relevance to the current WHO Framework for Health Emergency Preparedness and Response Capabilities; GHS indicators were used in sensitivity analyses.[^fanelli-2026a-methods-12]

### Limitations

**Scarcity of land mobility data:** Reliable data on cross-border land mobility in Africa remain scarce; land mobility was therefore estimated from the best available continental dataset, combining multiple mobility sources and deriving land movements as the residual after discounting commercial air travel.[^fanelli-2026a-discussion-7] Sensitivity analyses reducing land mobility by 66% and 90% produced only limited changes in country rankings, supporting robustness, though uncertainty remains.[^fanelli-2026a-discussion-8]

**Restricted analysis to contiguous Africa:** The analysis was restricted to contiguous Africa because comparable data on maritime mobility are not available.[^fanelli-2026a-discussion-9]

**Commercial flights only:** Air-mediated importation risk only considered commercial passenger flights and did not account for humanitarian, military, or charter flights.[^fanelli-2026a-discussion-6]

**Homogeneous travel behaviour and exposure risk:** The assumption of homogeneous travel behaviour and exposure risk across travellers does not capture variation by age, occupation, socioeconomic conditions, purpose of travel, or individual exposure.[^fanelli-2026a-discussion-3]

**Under-ascertainment difficult to quantify:** Geographically heterogeneous under-ascertainment is difficult to quantify; the proportion of investigated alerts was used as a proxy, and results were found to be robust to alternative assumptions.[^fanelli-2026a-discussion-4]

From the time-varying analysis of the 2014–16 epidemic, exportation risk per reported source case decreased by 4.6% per week (95% CI: 1.7–7.4%; p = 0.0097), and was substantially lower after the epidemic peak (0.46; 95% CI: 0.17–0.73 exported cases per 1,000 source cases) than before it (1.7; 95% CI: 0.95–2.7 exported cases per 1,000 source cases).[^van-zandvoort-2026-decrease-in-exportation-risk-over-the-3]

### Methods

**Historical case review and crude risk calculation:** The authors identified and analysed all known Ebola disease cases outside Africa to contextualise intercontinental spread, using manual and AI-assisted internet searches of scientific articles, public health bulletins, and news reports.[^van-zandvoort-2026-summary-3] As a crude risk measure, they calculated the number of cases occurring outside Africa per 1,000 reported cases in the originating epidemic for outbreaks since 2000, producing separate estimates including and excluding medically evacuated cases.[^van-zandvoort-2026-low-overall-risk-of-exportation-1] This calculation applies globally across all destination countries outside Africa combined, with a UK-specific example provided at the national level.

**Time-varying Poisson regression:** For cases exported from the 2014–16 West African epidemic specifically, the authors conducted a time-varying risk analysis using Poisson regression of the weekly count of exported cases by country (Guinea, Liberia, Sierra Leone) against the number of cases reported that week and a linear time trend.[^van-zandvoort-2026-decrease-in-exportation-risk-over-the-1] This produced an estimate of how exported case counts scaled with source-country incidence, with no significant deviation from linear scaling found.[^van-zandvoort-2026-decrease-in-exportation-risk-over-the-2] This method applies to the 2014–16 epidemic as a historical comparator and is not applied directly to the 2026 BVD outbreak.

### Assumptions

**Linear scaling of exportation risk with source incidence:** The Poisson regression assumes that exported case counts scale linearly with weekly source-country incidence, an assumption the analysis found consistent with the 2014–16 data (estimated scaling: 0.98; 95% CI: 0.60–1.36; p = 0.86).[^van-zandvoort-2026-decrease-in-exportation-risk-over-the-2] This assumption underpins the use of crude per-1,000-cases risk ratios to project forward.

**Crude risk ratio as applicable across outbreaks:** The crude risk measure is calculated for Ebola disease outbreaks since the year 2000[^van-zandvoort-2026-low-overall-risk-of-exportation-4], implicitly assuming that the historical ratio of exported-to-source cases is informative for the current 2026 BVD outbreak. The analysis does not explicitly state whether this assumption holds given differences in travel patterns, response capacity, or the specific virus species involved.

**Distinction between medically evacuated and latent cases:** The analysis distinguishes between medically evacuated cases, who represent known risks manageable with strict biosecurity, and latent cases arriving via commercial flights who need to be diagnosed before isolation.[^van-zandvoort-2026-discussion-2] The risk ratios are computed separately for these two categories, with the latent-only figure treated as the more policy-relevant measure of uncontrolled importation risk.

**Completeness of historical case ascertainment:** Agreement among search methods and with a prior clinical review supports that the case list is likely complete, but potential under-ascertainment may arise from underrepresentation of public health bulletins not published in English, and from possible geographic biases in web-based search tools.[^van-zandvoort-2026-discussion-3]

### Limitations

**Potential under-ascertainment of exported cases:** Public health bulletins not published in English were underrepresented despite targeted native-language searches, and web-based search tools may have returned geographically biased results, potentially leading to missed cases.[^van-zandvoort-2026-discussion-4]

**Historical data may not reflect current outbreak conditions:** The analysis applies risk ratios derived primarily from the 2014–16 West African epidemic — a very large, prolonged epidemic with substantial international humanitarian response — to the 2026 BVD context. The authors note that the decrease in exportation risk over the 2014–16 epidemic is likely most consistent with improvement in infection prevention and control measures coinciding with the stepping-up of response efforts in late 2014[^van-zandvoort-2026-decrease-in-exportation-risk-over-the-5], but do not formally assess whether this dynamic applies to the current, structurally different outbreak.

**Per-country risks not estimated:** The crude risk estimate represents the empirically observed risk across all countries outside Africa; per-country risks are smaller[^van-zandvoort-2026-low-overall-risk-of-exportation-5], and the analysis provides only the aggregate outside-Africa figure plus a single UK example, without ranking or estimating risk for individual destination countries.

**Latent case detection depends on screening:** The four latent cases identified occurred among 300,000 screened travellers and were all asymptomatic at exit and entry screening; their eventual identification relied on screening protocols that included guidance on self-monitoring of symptoms.[^van-zandvoort-2026-discussion-5] The analysis does not assess what the latent case rate would have been in the absence of such protocols.

### Data sources

**Primary outbreak data:** The analysis identified 28 confirmed Ebola disease cases outside Africa in total, of which 27 occurred during the 2014–16 West African epidemic and one has occurred so far during the 2026 BVD epidemic.[^van-zandvoort-2026-ebola-disease-cases-outside-africa-6] Sources consulted included 27 scientific articles, 9 public health bulletins, 82 news articles, and 9 other sites.[^van-zandvoort-2026-ebola-disease-cases-outside-africa-7]

**Auxiliary data:** Weekly case counts for Guinea, Liberia, and Sierra Leone (total 28,592 confirmed, probable, and suspected cases) covering the 2014–16 epidemic were used as the source-country incidence series in the Poisson regression.[^van-zandvoort-2026-decrease-in-exportation-risk-over-the-4] The authors also reference the approximately 300,000 travellers screened by CDC-supported exit screening programmes in Guinea, Liberia, and Sierra Leone during the 2014–16 epidemic, as context for the four identified latent cases.[^van-zandvoort-2026-discussion-1] Findings were checked against existing lists of Ebola disease cases.[^van-zandvoort-2026-ebola-disease-cases-outside-africa-8]
